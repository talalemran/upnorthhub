Amazon's average seller fees climbed from 27% of revenue in 2014 to roughly 50% in 2025 once referral fees, FBA fulfillment, advertising, and storage are all counted. Meanwhile, launching an independent online store now costs as little as $29–$79 a month plus standard payment processing. That gap — half your revenue on one path, a fixed low monthly cost on the other — is exactly why this question keeps coming up, and why the honest answer is genuinely more nuanced than "just build your own store to avoid the fees."

This article breaks down what selling on Amazon actually costs in 2026, what building your own store actually requires, and the sequencing decision that matters more than picking one permanently: which one should come first.

## How We Evaluated This Guide

Fee and margin figures here reflect current 2026 Amazon seller policy and independent industry analysis, cross-checked across multiple sources given how frequently Amazon's fee structure has changed within the year itself. We focused specifically on the sequencing question — which to start with — rather than treating this as a permanent either-or choice, since most successful sellers eventually use both channels rather than picking one forever.

## What Selling on Amazon Actually Costs in 2026

<img src="/images/articles/should-you-sell-on-amazon-or-build-your-own-store-first/01.webp" alt="amazon logo" loading="lazy" width="1200" height="675" />

Amazon's fee structure has grown more complex and more expensive over the past two years, and 2026 brought several new charges worth knowing about before committing capital to the platform.

**The core fees every seller pays:**

- **Subscription fee** — $0.99 per item on the Individual plan, or $39.99/month on the Professional plan.
- **Referral fee** — typically 8% to 17% of the sale price depending on category, though it runs as high as 45% in some categories, with most categories carrying a $0.30 minimum.
- **FBA fulfillment fee** — starting at $3.65 per unit for small standard-size items, scaling up by size and weight tier.
- **New for 2026:** a 3.5% fuel and logistics surcharge added April 17, inbound placement fees running up to $6.50 per unit, and inbound defect fees now ranging from $0.32 to $8.25 per unit for labeling or routing mistakes — errors that used to cost pennies and can now erase an entire inventory cycle's margin on a large shipment.

Add it up, and many sellers are now paying 35–45% of product revenue directly back to Amazon before a single dollar of marketing spend even enters the picture. Own-store businesses, by comparison, average 30–50% profit margins, while marketplace sellers typically see 10–20% margins after fees.

> "The real FBA calculation isn't about comparing per-unit fulfillment fees to your own shipping costs. It's about conversion — FBA listings with the Prime badge convert at 18–25% blended, compared to 10–13% for non-Prime." — Feedvisor, 2026 FBA analysis

## Why Sellers Pay Amazon's Fees Anyway

The fee structure looks brutal in isolation, but it's paired with something a brand-new store genuinely cannot replicate: instant access to buyer intent. The Buy Box — the primary purchase button on a product page — captures 82–85% of all Amazon sales, and it favors FBA sellers by 30–50% over self-fulfilled listings at the same price point. Prime members, now over 200 million strong, spend roughly three times what non-Prime shoppers do.

That's the real trade Amazon is selling: you're not just paying for fulfillment, you're paying for access to an enormous pool of ready-to-buy customers who already trust the platform and already have a saved payment method. A brand-new store has none of that built in — every visitor has to be earned through paid ads, SEO, or existing audience, none of which convert as reliably as an already-primed Amazon shopper clicking "Buy Now."

**Best for validating whether a product sells at all:** Amazon, since the built-in traffic gets you real sales data faster than building an audience from zero ever could.

## What Building Your Own Store Actually Requires

<img src="/images/articles/should-you-sell-on-amazon-or-build-your-own-store-first/02.webp" alt="keyboard with a cart logo" loading="lazy" width="1200" height="600" />

The cost side of an independent store has genuinely improved. Modern platforms run $29–$79 a month plus 2.5–3% payment processing, a small fraction of Amazon's effective take rate. But the honest trade is traffic: nobody is searching your store by default the way they're already searching Amazon.

The upside for the effort is real and compounding. Own stores don't just keep more margin — they own the customer relationship entirely. Email lists built from a direct store generate 30–40% of total revenue for businesses that build them well, at a reported $36 returned per $1 spent — a value marketplaces structurally cannot offer, since Amazon owns the customer data and relationship, not the seller.

That ownership difference shows up in business valuation too: direct-to-consumer businesses typically sell for 3–5 times annual profit, compared to 2–3 times for Amazon-dependent businesses. An acquirer is paying more for a business they know won't disappear the moment a platform changes its policy or algorithm.

**Best for building long-term brand equity and a sellable asset:** your own store, where customer data, email relationships, and pricing control compound in ways a marketplace listing never will.

## The Sequencing Question: Which Comes First

This is the actual decision that matters more than "Amazon vs. own store" as a permanent binary — most successful sellers eventually run both, so the real question is which to build first.

**Start with Amazon if:** you're testing an unproven product and need fast validation before committing real marketing budget. Amazon's built-in traffic gets you real sales data — conversion rate, return rate, actual demand — within weeks rather than the months it can take to build enough traffic to your own store to read the same signals reliably.

**Start with your own store if:** you already have some audience, existing traffic source, or a product with a strong enough brand story that differentiation matters more than instant marketplace traffic. A commodity-style product with nothing distinguishing it from competitors gets steamrolled in Amazon's price-driven search results just as easily as it would in an SEO-driven store with no existing audience.

**The pattern that shows up most often among sellers who do both well:** validate the product on Amazon first, using the data from real sales to refine positioning, pricing, and audience — then build the owned store once there's proven demand and some capital to invest in driving traffic to it deliberately, rather than guessing at product-market fit from zero traffic on both channels simultaneously.

## Where This Advice Falls Short

A few honest limitations belong here, since this decision genuinely depends on specifics this guide can't know about your product.

- **Category matters enormously and isn't captured in an average.** Amazon's referral fee alone ranges from 8% to 45% depending on category — a general fee average can misrepresent your specific product's real economics significantly.
- **Amazon's fee structure keeps changing, often mid-year.** The April 2026 fuel surcharge and expanded inbound defect fees are recent examples — treat any specific dollar figure here as directional, and verify current rates through Amazon's own Revenue Calculator before committing inventory capital.
- **A commodity product struggles on both channels, not just one.** Neither Amazon nor an independent store fixes a product with no real differentiation — channel choice doesn't substitute for product-market fit.
- **Building an owned store's traffic takes longer than this comparison might suggest.** The margin and valuation advantages of a direct store are real, but they assume you can actually drive meaningful traffic to it — a store with no visitors captures none of those benefits regardless of its superior margin structure.

## The Bottom Line

There's no universal right answer to Amazon versus your own store — there's a right sequence for your specific situation. Amazon's fees have grown steep enough that treating it as a permanent home base is a real cost to weigh carefully, but its built-in traffic remains unmatched for fast product validation. Your own store offers dramatically better margins, full customer ownership, and a business that's worth more when you eventually sell it — but only once you can actually drive traffic to it. The sellers doing this well in 2026 aren't choosing one forever. They're using Amazon to validate fast and their own store to build value that compounds.

## FAQ

**Is it cheaper to sell on Amazon or build your own store?**

Your own store, by a wide margin on paper — Amazon's average seller fees now run 35–50% of revenue once all charges are counted, compared to a fixed $29–$79 monthly cost plus 2.5–3% payment processing for an independent store.

**Why do sellers still use Amazon despite the high fees?**

Because of instant access to buyer intent — the Buy Box captures 82–85% of Amazon sales and favors FBA listings by 30–50% over self-fulfilled ones, giving new products fast validation that would take much longer to achieve from a cold start on an independent store.

**Should a brand-new business start on Amazon or build a store first?**

For an unproven product, Amazon is generally the faster path to real sales data; for a product with a strong brand story or existing audience, starting with an owned store can make more sense since differentiation matters more than instant marketplace traffic.

**Can you sell on both Amazon and your own store at the same time?**

Yes, and it's the most common pattern among established sellers — many validate a product on Amazon first, then build an owned store once demand is proven and there's capital to invest in driving traffic to it deliberately.

**Why do direct-to-consumer businesses sell for more than Amazon-dependent ones?**

Because owned customer data, email relationships, and full brand control make the business less dependent on a single platform's policies — DTC businesses typically sell for 3–5 times annual profit, compared to 2–3 times for Amazon-dependent sellers.
