Writing a blog post takes an average of 3 hours and 20 minutes, according to Orbit Media's 2026 blogging survey of more than 1,000 content marketers, and that's for a post averaging about 1,312 words. That's the time cost for one article, before research on a hard topic, editing, or promotion. For a business that needs several articles a month, the real question isn't whether writing is worth doing. It's who in the company should be spending those hours.

Here are three reasons companies outsource article writing: it converts a fixed cost into a variable one, it gives access to subject expertise you can't easily hire, and it makes consistent publishing realistic. The case isn't one-sided, though. Outsourcing isn't automatically cheaper per article, and each reason comes with conditions. This guide shows both sides, with current rate data.

## How We Evaluated This Guide

Rate figures come from the Editorial Freelancers Association's 2026 rate chart (a survey of more than 1,100 members about work done in 2025) and a January 2026 survey of 500 active freelance writers, plus several roundups. Some figures arrive secondhand through vendor sites with a commercial interest, such as writing services and AI content tools, and we flag those. Rates vary widely by niche and experience, so treat every number as a range to test against quotes from real writers.

## Reason One: It Turns a Fixed Cost Into a Variable One

The strongest financial argument isn't that a freelancer is cheaper per article. It's that you pay for articles, not for capacity.

An in-house writer is a fixed commitment. One 2026 cost breakdown estimates recruitment at about $5,475 per hire (using SHRM's 2025 average) and onboarding around $1,830 before the writer produces anything, on top of salary, benefits, and tools. Those figures come from a staffing vendor, so treat them as directional. The structural point holds either way: a salaried writer costs the same in a slow month as a busy one.

Outsourcing flips that. You pay per piece, often 50% upfront and the rest on delivery, which makes costs predictable and scalable. If you need four articles one month and ten the next, you adjust the order instead of the headcount.

Now the honest arithmetic. If your staff writes an average post in 3 hours 20 minutes and their time is worth $50 an hour, that's about $167 in labor for one article, which is less than a typical $250 to $399 freelance quote. Outsourcing doesn't win by being cheaper per piece in that case.

It wins when the person doing the writing has a higher-value use of their time. For a founder, marketer, or subject expert whose hours are worth $100 or more, 3 hours 20 minutes is $333 or more of opportunity cost per article, and handing writing to a specialist frees that time for work only they can do.

**Best for companies where writers are expensive or busy:** compare the freelance quote against what the in-house person would otherwise do with that time, not against their hourly pay alone.

## Reason Two: It Gives You Access to Expertise You Can't Easily Hire

A generalist can write clearly about anything. A specialist can write credibly about something specific. One rate guide notes that a writer who understands fintech regulation, SaaS go-to-market strategy, or enterprise security can produce content a generalist can't write convincingly, regardless of writing skill.

That matters because the market prices expertise explicitly. Per-word rates commonly run $0.05 to $0.20 at the entry level, $0.20 to $0.50 for mid-level writers, and $0.50 to $1.25 for experienced specialists, with some premium niches higher. Technology and SaaS writing is reported at $0.25 to $1.50 per word, and healthcare and finance carry premiums for regulatory knowledge. Hiring a full-time employee with that depth in every topic you cover is rarely practical, but you can rent it article by article.

> "A writer who understands the nuances of fintech regulation, SaaS go-to-market strategy, or enterprise security can produce content that a generalist cannot write convincingly, regardless of writing skill." — Ruul, 2026 freelance writer rates guide

This reason has a real condition attached. A specialist writer still needs your input: a clear brief, access to a subject-matter expert for an interview, and your own data or examples. Outsourced content that's generic usually reflects a generic brief, not a bad writer.

**Best for companies covering several distinct topics:** pick writers per topic area instead of expecting one hire to cover everything, and budget for the subject-matter time they'll need from your team.

## Reason Three: It Makes Consistent Publishing Realistic

Content results depend on consistency, and consistency depends on capacity. Earlier benchmark data points the same way: brands publishing weekly see meaningfully more conversions than monthly publishers, and creators who post steadily earn far more engagement per post than sporadic ones. Those figures describe correlation, not guaranteed causation, but they explain why a content program that stalls whenever the team gets busy underperforms.

Outsourcing protects the schedule. A freelance writer or small bench of writers works to deadlines and isn't pulled into product launches, customer calls, or other priorities the way an internal team member is. You also scale output without a hiring cycle, whether that's ramping up for a campaign or pausing when budgets tighten.

The 2026 landscape adds another option to weigh. Several vendors now sell AI-driven article engines at a flat monthly price, which changes the cost comparison for basic, high-volume content. AI drafting is widespread among marketers, but a drafted article still needs expert input, fact-checking, and a point of view to stand out. For many companies the practical answer is a mix: AI-assisted drafting for routine pieces, and human specialists for expert-led content.

**Best for teams whose publishing keeps slipping:** treat consistency as the goal, and outsource enough volume that the calendar holds even when internal priorities shift.

## How to Outsource Article Writing Well

Outsourcing works when you manage it, not when you hand it off and hope. A short process helps:

1. **Write a real brief.** Include the audience, goal, target keyword or question, key points, sources, and examples of tone you like.
2. **Commission a paid trial.** Test one or two articles before committing to a volume, and judge the result against your brief.
3. **Define ownership and revisions up front.** Agree on who owns the work, how many revision rounds are included, and how approvals flow.
4. **Provide expert access.** Give writers a way to interview your subject-matter experts or review facts.
5. **Keep an editor.** Someone internal should check accuracy, brand voice, and claims before publishing.
6. **Measure outcomes, not just output.** Track traffic, leads, and conversions from the content, not only how many articles were delivered.

## When Not to Outsource

Outsourcing isn't always the right call. Keep writing in-house when:

- **The content depends on deep, proprietary knowledge** that would take a writer months to absorb.
- **Your volume is high and steady**, since a salaried writer can become cheaper per piece at sustained volume.
- **Brand voice is central and still forming**, and you need tight control over it.
- **You don't have time to brief and review**, since poorly managed outsourcing produces generic work.

## Where This Guide Falls Short

A few honest limitations apply.

- **Rate data varies widely.** Benchmarks range from $0.05 per word for beginners to $2 or more for experts, and surveys differ on averages, so quotes from real writers matter more than any survey.
- **Some figures come from interested parties.** Staffing firms, writing services, and AI-content vendors publish much of the cost data, and they benefit from particular conclusions.
- **Per-article savings aren't guaranteed.** As the arithmetic shows, outsourcing can cost more per piece than internal writing when the internal writer's time is cheap, so the benefit is usually fixed-cost avoidance and opportunity cost.
- **Quality depends on management.** The best-case benefits only appear with a strong brief, expert input, and editorial review.

## The Bottom Line

Companies outsource article writing for three solid reasons. It converts a fixed cost into a variable one, so you pay for output instead of capacity. It provides subject expertise you can rent per topic. And it makes steady publishing realistic when internal teams are stretched. It isn't automatically cheaper per article, and it works only when you brief well, supply expert input, and keep an editor in the loop. Use the freelance rate benchmarks to set a budget, test with a paid trial, and judge the result on the outcomes the content produces.

## FAQ

**How much does it cost to outsource an article?**

A typical 1,500-word blog post runs about $250 to $399 from the average freelancer, and about $375 to $675 at EFA median rates, with specialists charging more.

**Is outsourcing article writing cheaper than hiring a writer?**

Not necessarily per article, since writing a post takes about 3 hours 20 minutes on average. The savings come from avoiding fixed costs like recruitment, onboarding, and salary, and from freeing expert time.

**How do I get good quality from outsourced writers?**

Write a detailed brief, commission a paid trial, give writers access to your subject-matter experts, and keep an internal editor to check accuracy and brand voice.

**Should I use AI instead of a human writer?**

AI can draft routine content cheaply, but it still needs expert input, fact-checking, and a point of view, so many companies combine AI-assisted drafting with human specialists for expert-led pieces.

**When should a company keep writing in-house?**

When content depends on deep proprietary knowledge, when output is high and steady enough that a salary is cheaper per piece, or when brand voice needs tight control.
